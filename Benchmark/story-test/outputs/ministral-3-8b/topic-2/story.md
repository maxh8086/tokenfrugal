**"The Unmarked Cargo"**

The northern corridor was supposed to be routine. Captain Ines Varga had flown it for six years, her drone humming smoothly through the endless sky, its cargo hold carrying only medical supplies and spare parts—items she had signed for, items she knew by weight, by size, by the familiar weight of duty. But on that Tuesday, as the drone ascended into the cold blue expanse, the load sensor flickered to life with an anomaly.

Four sealed boxes. Four boxes that weren’t on the manifest.

The dispatcher’s screen labeled them *"reserved cargo."* No origin. No destination. Just a quiet, unspoken presence in the hold. Ines’s fingers tightened on the controls. Something was wrong. Something had been slipped into her route without a word.

She checked the seal numbers. They were real—official, tampered with only by the depot’s automated system. She cross-referenced the flight log, noting every timestamp, every deviation from protocol. The boxes were cold to the touch, their surfaces smooth and unmarked, save for a faint, rhythmic hum that seemed to vibrate through the drone’s frame, a low, steady pulse like a heartbeat.

At the next depot, she pulled into the loading bay and stepped out, her boots crunching on frost. The loader—a broad-shouldered man with a cigarette dangling from his lips—shrugged when she asked about the boxes. *"Not my job to ask,"* he muttered, already turning back to his work. She tried the dispatcher next. The woman behind the screen didn’t even look up from her terminal. *"Route’s been approved at a higher level,"* she said, her voice flat. *"You’re cleared to fly."*

Ines stood there, the weight of the unknown pressing against her ribs. She was the one flying the aircraft. She was the one who would answer if something went wrong.

That night, back in the cockpit, she filed a report. It was short. It was honest. She detailed the boxes, their seals, their temperature, the hum that seemed to seep into the drone’s systems. She sent it and waited.

No reply came.

---

The next leg of the route took her over the frozen tundra, where the wind howled like a living thing. The boxes remained silent, their presence a ghost in the hold. Ines kept her hands on the controls, her mind racing. Who had added them? Why? And what were they carrying?

She thought of the last time she’d encountered something unexplained—a missing shipment of vaccines, a cargo hold that had been "misaligned" by the system. Both times, the higher-ups had brushed it off. *"Operational oversight,"* they’d said. *"Human error."*

But this wasn’t error. This was something else.

By the third day, the hum had grown louder. It wasn’t just in the boxes anymore. It was in the drone’s frame, a vibration that made the instruments flicker. Ines checked the diagnostics. Nothing. The system was stable. *Too stable.*

She landed at the next depot, her stomach tight. The loader was different this time—younger, nervous. He didn’t meet her eyes when she asked about the boxes. *"Just… keep flying,"* he muttered.

Ines didn’t.

She pulled up the manifest again, her fingers flying over the terminal. There was no record of the boxes being removed. No note, no transfer. Just… them. Waiting.

She made another report. This one was longer. She included the hum, the diagnostics, the way the boxes seemed to *react* to the drone’s systems. She sent it and waited.

Still nothing.

---

The final leg of the route was the longest. The drone climbed higher, the air thinning around her. The boxes hummed now, a steady, insistent rhythm, like a machine waking from sleep. Ines’s hands shook on the controls. She had never felt so alone.

She thought of the last time she’d questioned something. The time she’d asked about the "unaccounted for" medical supplies that had vanished mid-flight. The time she’d been told to *"stop digging."*

She didn’t stop this time.

She landed at the final depot, her pulse hammering in her ears. The loader was gone. The bay was empty. Only the boxes remained, their hum filling the silence.

Ines stepped closer. She ran her gloved fingers over the surface. Cold. Smooth. No labels. No markings.

Then she saw it—a single, faint mark, almost invisible, pressed into the metal near the seal. A symbol. A triangle. A circle. A line.

She had seen it before.

She had seen it in the reports of the missing cargo. In the whispers of the depot workers. In the eyes of the dispatchers who knew too much.

She pulled out her terminal and called the only number she trusted.

The line rang once. Twice.

Then a voice, rough and urgent: *"Ines. You’re not supposed to be here."*

She didn’t answer. She just sent the final report.

And then she flew.

**"The Last Drop"**

The drought had turned Port Marrow into a city of shadows. Three years without rain, and the rooftop gardens of Grey Stair had withered under the relentless sun. Mara Ellis wiped her brow as she watered the last of the basil plants, her fingers brushing against the cracked earth. The shared tank—once a symbol of resilience—now felt like a fragile lifeline, its concrete walls straining under the weight of scarcity.

Then came the letter.

Mara unfolded it carefully, her stomach tightening as she read the crisp, official paper. *Lindqvist Partners* had purchased the rights to the rooftop cistern. The words were polite, almost apologetic: *"Managed going forward. Access reviewed in due course."* She crumpled the letter in her fist. They were being evicted from their own water.

---

The Thursday meeting was a storm in miniature.

The co-op’s small kitchen hummed with tension as twelve faces crowded around the tea urn, steam curling into the stale air. Old Tobin, his gnarled hands resting on the table, leaned forward. "I told you this would happen. Developers don’t care about gardens—they care about profit." Priya, sharp-eyed and quick to anger, slammed her palm down. "We need a lawyer. Now." The room fell silent, waiting for Mara to speak.

She took a breath. "We have the key to the tank. The pump receipts. Nine years of meter readings." Her voice was steady, but her hands trembled as she laid the papers on the table. "That’s something."

A murmur rippled through the group. For the first time in months, they felt something like hope.

---

The next step was bravery.

They took photos—close-ups of the rusted meters, the faded receipts, the handwritten notes tracking every drop. They copied the papers, traced the timeline of their shared water, and pinned it to the stairwell wall. By Friday, the entire street knew. Whispers spread like wildfire: *The co-op’s fighting back.*

Mara stood on the fire escape, staring at the tank looming above. It had held them through storms and shortages, through the arguments and the quiet understanding that they were in this together. Now, someone else wanted to take it.

She thought of the children who had splashed in the shallow pool they’d built in the garden. Of the elderly neighbor who had watered her ferns with trembling hands. Of the way the whole building had leaned on each other when the drought first hit.

This wasn’t just about water.

It was about belonging.

And they weren’t giving it up without a fight.

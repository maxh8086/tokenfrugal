**The Last Drop**

The sun baked Port Marrow into a city of dust and hollows. Mara Ellis knelt in the cracked earth of Grey Stair’s rooftop, her calloused hands pressing water into the parched soil. The basil leaves curled under her touch—another loss to the endless dryness. The shared tank, once their lifeline, now groaned under the weight of their desperation. The concrete groaned too, splitting along its seams like old skin.

A knock at the door made her jump. She wiped her brow and opened it to find Old Tobin standing there, his breath ragged. "Letter came for you," he rasped, pressing a folded sheet into her palm. The paper smelled of ink and something sharper—fear. *Lindqvist Partners* had bought the cistern. The words cut deeper than the drought. *"Managed going forward."* They were being pushed out of their own water.

---

The kitchen smelled of stale tea and unspoken anger. Priya’s fingers drummed against the table, her jaw tight. "We can’t just let them take it," she snapped. Old Tobin’s voice was rough with years of silence broken. "They’ll turn it into some corporate pool, and we’ll be left with nothing." Mara’s hands shook as she slid the papers across the table—the key, the receipts, the years of meter readings. "We’ve got proof. Every drop."

A silence settled, thick as the dust outside. Then, slowly, Priya exhaled. "So we fight."

---

They worked by moonlight. The photos developed in a stolen kitchen, the meters traced with trembling hands. The stairwell became a war room, the wall now lined with dates, numbers, and names—every tenant who had ever poured water, every time the pump had sputtered. By dawn, the entire block knew. The tank wasn’t just concrete and rust. It was theirs.

Mara stood on the fire escape, staring up at the hulking cistern. She remembered the children laughing in the shallow pool they’d carved from the overflow. The old woman’s ferns, watered with shaking hands. The way the building had held them through the drought—not as strangers, but as people who chose each other.

They weren’t just defending a tank.

They were defending the way they lived.

And they wouldn’t go quietly.

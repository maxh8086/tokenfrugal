**"The Woman Who Knew the Sky"**

The air in Harrow Creek was thick with the kind of quiet that only comes after midnight. The town slept, its streets empty except for the occasional car passing like a ghost through the dark. Inside the WKRV radio station, Dale Ransome sat behind the desk, the glow of his old radio equipment casting long shadows across the studio. It was his show—*Midnight Caller*—and for eleven years, he had been the only voice people trusted when the world outside grew too quiet.

Dale took every call seriously. Even the strange ones.

The phone rang at 12:14 AM.

"Hello, WKRV," Dale said, his voice steady despite the late hour.

There was a pause. Then a woman’s voice, soft but clear, said, "My name is June."

Dale frowned. "June, you're calling about the weather?"

She didn’t hesitate. "Tomorrow. The rain starts at the water tower at six in the morning. The wind turns east by noon. And the hail comes just after three."

Dale’s fingers tightened around the phone. The forecast sheet on his desk read *clear skies*. He glanced at it again, as if the ink might change. "That’s… unusual."

June didn’t laugh. She didn’t sound nervous. She sounded like someone reading a grocery list. "You’ll see."

Dale exhaled. "Well, June, thanks for the tip. We’ll keep it in mind."

He hung up.

The studio was silent except for the hum of the old equipment. Dale stared at the phone, then at the forecast sheet. He didn’t believe in coincidences. But he didn’t believe in ghosts, either.

---

The next morning, Dale arrived at the station early, his coffee cold in his hands. The sky was still blue when he stepped outside, but the air smelled different—like rain waiting just beyond the horizon. He checked his watch: 5:58 AM.

Then he heard it.

A distant rumble.

Dale turned toward the water tower, a tall, rusted structure at the edge of town. The clouds above it were darkening, swallowing the morning light. At 6:02 AM, the first drops fell.

He watched, stunned, as the rain began—not in sheets, but in a slow, deliberate pattern, as if someone were turning a faucet just so. By 6:05, the streets were wet.

The forecast sheet was wrong.

By noon, the wind had shifted. Dale stepped outside again, feeling the gusts push against his face. Sure enough, the breeze came from the east, exactly as June had said. He turned to the station, where the weatherman’s voice crackled over the airwaves, still insisting on *clear skies*.

At 3:08 PM, the sky split open.

Hailstones the size of quarters struck the ground with a sharp *clack-clack-clack*. Dale ducked, shielding his face as they pelted the car parked in the station lot. The wind howled, and the rain turned to something heavier, something violent. By 3:10, the hail was falling in earnest.

Dale sat in his car, watching the storm roll over Harrow Creek like a living thing. He had no explanation. He didn’t need one.

---

June never called back.

The station received a flood of emails—*Did she know something we didn’t? Was she a meteorologist? A psychic?*—and a single letter, postmarked from nowhere, addressed only to Dale. He kept it in his desk drawer, next to the old forecast sheet, its contents unopened.

He never again said the word *clear* on air.

Some nights, when the radio was quiet and the world outside was still, Dale would pick up the phone and dial an unused number. He’d wait. He’d listen.

But June never answered.

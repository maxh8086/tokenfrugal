The late-night radio landscape was a peculiar ecosystem, a solitary frequency drifting through the quiet of the small inland town of Harrow Creek. In this town, where the streetlights flickered against the mist and the silence between cars was heavy with unspoken thoughts, WKRV's midnight show was the only voice many people trusted after dark. For eleven years, host Dale Ransome had been that voice. He was a man of routine, a man who knew the cadence of the town's sleep. It was important to note that Dale took every call seriously, even the strange ones. He treated the static-filled voices of the insomniac as confessions, complaints, or confessions of love, never dismissing a caller as a prankster.

The call came in at 12:14 AM. The line crackled with the sound of rain, though the sky above the studio was a deep, unbroken indigo. The caller, a woman who gave her name only as June, spoke with a voice that was neither young nor old, neither frantic nor weary. She said she could describe tomorrow's weather.

Dale was skeptical. Skepticism was a habit he had cultivated over a decade of hosting, a shield against the absurdity of human nature. But he let her talk. She said the rain would start at the water tower around six in the morning. She said the wind would turn east by noon. She said the hail would come just after three. She sounded calm, almost bored, as if she were reading a shopping list for a grocery store rather than predicting a storm.

Dale felt a shift in the room. The air conditioning hummed, a low vibration that usually soothed him, but tonight it felt like a warning. He checked the forecast sheet on his desk. It was printed in crisp black ink on white paper, a grid of blue and green symbols indicating clear skies. The National Weather Service had issued a statement: *Clear skies expected through the weekend.*

He thought about that for a while. He thought about the water tower, a rusting iron structure that stood like a sentinel on the hill overlooking the town. He thought about the wind, which currently blew from the west, carrying the scent of pine and damp earth. He thought about the hail, which was impossible in a clear sky. He looked at the clock. It was 12:15 AM. He had one hour before the show went live.

At the end of the day, he decided to play the call the next morning. He didn't want to bury it. He wanted to hear it again, to see if the static would change, to see if the voice would crack. He saved the recording to a tape labeled *June*, a label he had never used before.

The next day, the rain began at 6:02 AM. It did not start gently; it began with a sudden, violent drumming against the studio windows, turning the glass into a blur of grey. Dale watched the water tower from his kitchen window. The rusted metal groaned as the first drops hit it, a sound that echoed across the town like a bell tolling for a funeral no one had planned. By 6:15, the streets were slick, and the gutters began to overflow.

By eleven, the wind turned east. It was a cold, biting wind that whipped the leaves off the oak trees and drove the rain sideways. Dale stood on his porch, watching the clouds roll in from the ocean, a direction that made no sense for a town so far inland. The sky, which had been blue and cloudless at noon, was now a churning mass of grey and white.

At 3:10 PM, the hail came. It was not the soft, melting pellets of a spring storm, but heavy, jagged stones that shattered the windshields of parked cars and pattered against the roof of Dale's station. He sat in his car in the station lot, the engine idling, and watched the sky turn a bruised purple. He had no explanation. He did not pretend to have one. He did not say, *It was a freak storm,* or *The computer must have glitched,* or *June was a liar.* He simply sat there, listening to the thunder roll over the hills, and felt a profound, quiet respect for the woman who had called him at midnight.

June never called back. The station received a few emails, a flood of questions from people who wanted to know if Dale had been pranked, if he had been drunk, if the weather service had been hacked. There were conspiracy theories about government weather weapons and alien interference. There was one letter, handwritten on yellow legal pad paper, with no return address. It was addressed to Dale Ransome, but it contained no message, only a single sentence: *Thank you for listening.*

Dale kept the letter in his drawer, next to the old forecast sheet that still bore the words *Clear Skies*. He never again said the word "clear" on air. He stopped using the phrase, not because he feared the weather, but because he understood that the world was not a place that could be predicted, only experienced. He continued to host the show, taking every call seriously, even the strange ones, knowing that sometimes, the most important truths came from the people who sounded the most bored.

The northern corridor was a ribbon of light cutting through the perpetual twilight of the outer rim. For six years, Captain Ines Varga had flown this specific stretch of sky, memorizing the turbulence patterns over the ice fields and the precise altitude required to avoid the magnetic shear near the equatorial drift. In automated freight, routes were supposed to be simple algorithms, devoid of human error or moral ambiguity. The drone was a vessel of pure utility, designed to carry only medical stock and spare parts. Those were the items on the manifest, and those were the items she had signed for with her own biometric signature.

On the Tuesday run, the routine shattered with a soft chime from the load sensor. The screen flickered, displaying four sealed boxes that the system insisted were present in the cargo hold. They were not on the manifest. The dispatch screen labeled them simply as "reserved cargo," a bureaucratic euphemism that felt heavy in the quiet cabin. Ines felt a cold knot tightening in her stomach. Something was moving through her route, and nobody had told her.

She did not panic; she observed. She checked the seal numbers, cross-referencing the holographic tags against the central registry. The numbers matched nothing she had ever seen before. She checked the flight log, scrolling through the digital tape of the journey, looking for any anomaly in the navigation data. She wrote down every timestamp, noting the exact moment the boxes appeared in the sensor feed. The boxes themselves were cold to the touch, radiating a chill that seemed to seep into the floor plating. They hummed faintly, a steady, low-frequency vibration that seemed to fill the cabin, a sound that made the coffee in her cup ripple without a breeze.

At the next depot, a small station clinging to the edge of a frozen lake, she disembarked and asked the loader about them. The loader, a young man with grease under his fingernails and eyes that darted toward the exit, shrugged. "It's not my place to ask," he said, his voice flat. "The manifest says what it says. If the system says it's there, it's there."

She asked the dispatcher at the station, a woman who looked as though she hadn't slept in a week. The dispatcher tapped a few keys on a holographic interface and replied without looking up. "The route was approved at a higher level," she said, her tone suggesting that the word 'higher' was a shield against accountability. "You just fly the path. Don't question the payload."

Ines thought about that for a long time while she refueled the drone. She thought about the regulations she had memorized, the safety protocols she had drilled into her mind during her training. She thought about the fact that she was the one flying the aircraft, the one responsible for the lives of the medical supplies inside, and the one who would answer if something went wrong. If the boxes contained a virus, or a weapon, or something worse, the blame would rest on her shoulders. The system could not be wrong, the dispatcher had implied, but the system was a collection of people, and people made mistakes.

She returned to the cockpit, the hum of the boxes growing slightly louder as the drone accelerated. She filed a report. It was short, and it was honest. She listed the four boxes, noted their absence from the official manifest, recorded the temperature readings, and described the humming sound. She attached a photo of the seal numbers. She did not add any speculation, for speculation was a liability. She simply stated the facts as she perceived them.

Then she sat in the cockpit and waited for a reply that did not come. The drone climbed into the upper atmosphere, where the air was thin and the stars were sharp and unblinking. Below her, the world continued its indifferent rotation, unaware of the cargo that was not supposed to be there. Ines watched the horizon, her hands steady on the controls, wondering if the silence of the comms channel was a sign of approval or a sign of erasure. The route was simple, the algorithm was clear, and yet, as she flew through the dark, she realized that the most dangerous thing in the sky was not the turbulence or the ice, but the quiet certainty that someone, somewhere, had decided that the rules did not apply to her.

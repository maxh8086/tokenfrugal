Inference chain

The fleet planner at Corvane Logistics was built to reason through complex routing problems, and it did so in a clear, step-by-step way. It is worth noting that its reasoning was only as good as the rules it inherited. Step one of the chain read: the depot at Eastmoor has a loading capacity of forty pallets per shift.

The planner used that assumption to reroute twelve trucks away from the coast road. Step two followed from step one. If Eastmoor could take forty pallets, then the overflow could move to Western Yard. Step three followed from step two: the Western Yard route was shorter, so the fleet should switch immediately. The chain was tidy, and it seemed robust.

Analyst Renata Osei read the output on Monday morning. Something felt off. She navigated the complexities of the old rule book, page by page, until she found the original note from six years earlier. Eastmoor's capacity was forty pallets per shift, yes, but only on the north dock. The south dock had been closed since a flood. The planner had no way to know that.

She shed light on the error in a short memo. The planner's conclusion was not wrong in its logic. It was wrong in its first premise, and every later step inherited that mistake. At the end of the day, the chain of reasoning had been pivotal to the decision, and it had been built on a single false line in a document nobody reread.

Renata updated the rule, reran the planner, and watched the trucks turn back toward the coast road.

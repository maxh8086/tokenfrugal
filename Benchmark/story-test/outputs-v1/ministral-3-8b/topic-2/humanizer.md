**The Unmarked Cargo**

The drone sliced through the Arctic sky, six years of routine etched into its frame—medical supplies, spare parts, the quiet weight of duty pressing against Ines’s ribs. But today, the load sensor flickered. Four boxes. No record. Just a hum, low and insistent, vibrating through the drone’s metal like a buried pulse.

Ines traced the seal numbers with her thumb. Official. But the depot’s cold fingers had left no trace. She cross-referenced timestamps, watched the flight log scroll like a whispered secret. The boxes had been added without a trace. No origin. No destination. Just the hum, steady as a heartbeat beneath the metal.

The loader at the next stop exhaled smoke, his shoulders hunched against the wind. *"Not my problem,"* he muttered, already turning away. The dispatcher barely glanced up. *"Higher clearance,"* she said. *"You’re good."*

Ines stood in the loading bay, the unknown pressing against her chest. She was the one who’d fly. She was the one who’d answer if something broke.

That night, she typed her report—no fluff, no excuses. The boxes. The seals. The hum that seeped into the drone’s bones. She sent it.

No reply.

---

The tundra stretched below her, a jagged wound in the earth, the wind howling like a warning. The boxes remained. Silent. Watching. Ines clenched the controls, her mind racing. Who had added them? Why? What were they carrying?

She remembered the last time she’d questioned something—the missing vaccines, the "misaligned" cargo. Both times, the answer had been the same: *"Operational error."* But this wasn’t error. This was something else.

By the third day, the hum wasn’t just in the boxes anymore. It was in the drone’s frame, a vibration that made the instruments flicker like a dying light. Ines checked the diagnostics. Nothing. *Too* stable. Like a machine pretending to be alive.

She landed at the next depot. The loader was younger now, his hands shaking as he avoided her eyes. *"Just… keep flying,"* he whispered.

Ines didn’t.

She pulled up the manifest again. No record of the boxes being removed. No notes. No transfers. Just them. Waiting.

She wrote another report—longer this time. The hum. The diagnostics. The way the boxes seemed to *listen* to the drone’s systems. She sent it.

Still nothing.

---

The final leg stretched on, the air thin as a held breath. The boxes hummed now—a steady, insistent pulse, like a machine waking from hibernation. Ines’s fingers trembled on the controls. She had never felt so alone.

She thought of the last time she’d been told to stop digging. The time she’d been warned to let things go. She didn’t listen then. She wouldn’t now.

The depot was empty when she landed. Only the boxes remained, their hum filling the silence. Ines ran her gloved hand over their surface—cold, smooth, unlabeled. Then she saw it: a mark near the seal—a triangle, a circle, a line. She’d seen it before. In the reports of the missing cargo. In the way depot workers shifted when she asked questions. In the eyes of those who knew too much.

She pulled out her terminal and dialed. The line rang once. Twice.

A voice, rough with urgency: *"Ines. You’re not supposed to be here."*

She didn’t answer. She just sent the final report.

And then she flew.

**"The Midnight Caller"**

The night air wrapped around Harrow Creek like a living thing, heavy with the kind of silence that pressed against your ribs. The town slept, its streets empty except for the occasional car’s headlights slicing through the dark. Inside WKRV’s dim studio, Dale Ransome sat hunched over his desk, the radio’s old dials casting long, jagged shadows across the walls. His show, *Midnight Caller*, had been his life for eleven years—his voice the only one people trusted when the world outside grew too still.

The phone rang at 12:14 AM.

"WKRV," Dale answered, his voice steady, though his pulse quickened.

A pause. Then—a woman’s voice, soft but sharp, like a blade wrapped in silk. *"Tomorrow. The rain starts at the water tower at six. The wind shifts east by noon. Hail comes after three."*

Dale’s fingers tightened around the receiver. The forecast sheet on his desk promised *clear skies*. He stared at it, as if the numbers might rewrite themselves under his gaze. "That’s… impossible."

No laughter. No hesitation. Just a voice, flat and precise, like a clock ticking down. *"You’ll see."*

Dale exhaled. "Well. Thanks for the tip."

He hung up.

The studio hummed with the radio’s quiet breath. Dale stared at the phone, then at the forecast sheet. He didn’t believe in omens. He didn’t believe in ghosts.

---

Morning came too soon. Dale stepped outside, his coffee long gone cold. The sky was still blue, but the air smelled wrong—like rain pressing against the horizon, like something holding its breath. His watch read 5:58 AM.

Then he heard it.

A low, rolling growl.

He turned toward the water tower, its rusted silhouette looming at the edge of town. The clouds above it darkened, swallowing the light. At 6:02 AM, the first drops fell—not a storm, but a deliberate trickle, as if someone had just turned on a faucet.

By noon, the wind had turned. Dale stepped outside, feeling the gusts bite his face. The breeze came from the east, just as she’d said. The station’s weatherman still insisted on *clear skies*.

At 3:08 PM, the sky split.

Hailstones the size of quarters struck the ground with sharp, rhythmic *clacks*. Dale ducked, shielding his face as they pelted the station lot. The wind screamed. The rain turned to something heavier, something alive. By 3:10, the storm had arrived.

He sat in his car, watching Harrow Creek drown in something that wasn’t natural. The world outside wasn’t breaking—it was *listening*.

---

June never called again.

The station flooded with questions—*Was she a meteorologist? A psychic?*—and a single letter, postmarked from nowhere, addressed only to Dale. He kept it in his drawer, beside the crumpled forecast sheet. He’d never open it.

He stopped saying *clear* on air.

Some nights, when the radio was silent and the world outside held its breath, Dale would pick up the phone and dial a number that didn’t belong to anyone. He’d listen. He’d wait.

June never answered.

But sometimes, when the wind howled just right, he swore he heard her voice in the static—

*"You’ll see."*

In the quiet town of Harrow Creek, nestled between rolling hills and whispering woods, the late-night radio show on WKRV was a beacon of comfort. Host Dale Ransome, with his weathered voice and steady presence, had been the voice of the night for eleven years. His midnight forecast was a ritual, a promise of safety and predictability in the darkness. But on a particularly stormy night, something shifted.

The call came in at 12:14, the clock ticking ominously in the background. The caller, a woman who identified herself only as June, spoke with a calm, almost bored tone. "Tomorrow's weather," she said, her voice steady. "Rain at the water tower at six. Wind will turn east by noon. Hail just after three."

Dale Ransome listened, his skepticism warring with his curiosity. He checked the forecast sheet on his desk, which read "clear skies." He pondered for a moment, the studio humming softly around him. "June, can you tell me more?" he asked, his voice barely above a whisper.

June's response was immediate. "That's all I can say. Clear skies tonight, rain tomorrow."

Dale felt a shift in the room. He glanced at the clock, then back at the forecast sheet. He knew he should dismiss it, but June's tone, her certainty, made him hesitate. "Alright, June, we'll see," he said, his voice firm but tinged with uncertainty.

The next morning, Dale sat in his car in the station lot, watching the sky turn grey. The rain began at 6:02, exactly as June had predicted. The water tower, a landmark in the town, was the first to be drenched. By eleven, the wind had indeed turned east, and just after three, the hail began, pelting the ground with a sharp, relentless rhythm.

Dale watched, his mind racing. He had no explanation, and he did not pretend to have one. He simply sat, his car engine idling, as the storm raged on. The station received a few emails, a flood of questions, and one letter with no return address. Dale kept the letter in his drawer, next to the old forecast sheet, a silent testament to the night's events.

June never called back. The station's audience was abuzz with speculation, but Dale kept his silence. He had seen enough to know that sometimes, the world was not as predictable as it seemed. He never again said the word "clear" on air, opting instead for a more cautious, honest approach.

Years passed, and the memory of that night faded into the background of Dale's life. But every time the weather seemed too perfect, he would glance at the drawer, at the old forecast sheet, and the letter with no return address. And he would remember the night when the world had shifted, and the voice on the radio had known more than it should have.

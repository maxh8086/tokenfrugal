The sun hung low over Port Marrow, a coastal city that had forgotten the taste of rain. For three years, the drought had turned the sky into a relentless, shimmering sheet of heat. The air tasted of salt and dust, and the rooftop gardens of Grey Stair, once a riot of green and purple, were now a patchwork of brown earth and withered stems. It was a slow, silent death that every resident felt in the back of their throat.

At the heart of this struggle was the shared water tank. It was a massive concrete cistern perched on the roof, a silent giant that fed all twelve flats of the co-op. For decades, it had been the lifeblood of the building, a communal resource that bound the neighbors together. But today, the rhythm of their lives was about to be shattered.

Mara Ellis stood in the small kitchen of Flat 4, her hands trembling slightly as she read the letter for the second time. The envelope was crisp and white, bearing the logo of Lindqvist Partners, a developer known for turning old neighborhoods into sleek, sterile condos. The letter was polite, almost apologetic in its tone. It stated that the partners had purchased the rights to the tank and that access would be "managed going forward." The phrase "reviewed in due course" hung in the air like a threat. Mara felt a cold knot tighten in her stomach. The co-op had navigated property disputes, plumbing nightmares, and budget crises, but this felt different. This felt like a game-changer.

The Thursday meeting was held in the communal lounge, where the air was thick with the scent of stale tea and anxiety. Twelve people sat around the small table, their faces drawn and tired. Old Tobin, a man whose knees clicked whenever he stood up, was the first to speak. He leaned forward, his voice raspy but firm. "I've seen this coming," he said, tapping the letter with a calloused finger. "They don't want to share anymore. They want the water for themselves, or they want to sell it to the highest bidder."

Priya, the youngest member of the group, pushed her glasses up the bridge of her nose. "We need a lawyer," she insisted, her voice rising. "We need to sue them. We need to fight."

Mara said nothing for a long moment. She watched the faces around her, seeing the fear and the anger. Then, she spoke. Her voice was quiet, but it cut through the room like a knife. "We have the tank key," she said, placing the heavy brass key on the table. "We have the pump receipts from the last six months. We have nine years of meter readings. That counts for something."

The room went quiet. The silence was heavy, pressing against the ears. It was a testament to Mara's steady nature that the room settled into listening. She wasn't shouting; she was stating facts. "If we act now, we can prove we've been the stewards of this water," she continued. "We can show them that the co-op has always been responsible. We can't let them just take it."

They decided to shed light on the situation. They didn't have a lawyer yet, but they had each other. They spent the rest of the afternoon photographing the meters, copying the receipts, and gathering every scrap of paper that proved the co-op's history with the tank. They compiled a timeline, a visual story of their stewardship, and pinned it to the stairwell wall.

By the weekend, the whole street knew. The news spread like wildfire through the narrow alleys of Port Marrow. Neighbors who had never spoken to each other stopped on their doorsteps to discuss the letter. The drought had already made them dependent on one another; now, it had forged a new kind of bond. They were no longer just twelve strangers sharing a roof; they were a community facing a common enemy.

As the sun set on the third day of the week, Mara stood on the roof, looking down at the city. The tank was still there, a silent witness to their struggle. The knot in her stomach had loosened, replaced by a strange, fierce determination. They had the key, the receipts, and the truth. They were ready to fight.

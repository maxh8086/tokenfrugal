In the realm of automated freight, routes were supposed to be simple. Captain Ines Varga had flown the northern corridor for six years, and she knew every waypoint by heart. The drone was supposed to carry only medical stock and spare parts, items that were on the manifest and items she signed for.

On the Tuesday run, the drone's load sensor reported four sealed boxes she had never seen. They were not on the manifest. The dispatch screen showed them as "reserved cargo." Ines felt a shift in her routine. Something was moving through her route, and nobody had told her.

She navigated the complexities of the situation carefully. She checked the seal numbers. She checked the flight log. She wrote down every timestamp. The boxes were cold to the touch, and they hummed faintly, a steady sound that seemed to fill the cabin.

At the next depot, she asked the loader about them. He shrugged and said it was not his place to ask. She asked the dispatcher. The dispatcher said the route was approved at a higher level. Ines thought about that for a long time. At the end of the day, she was the one flying the aircraft, and she was the one who would answer if something went wrong.

She filed a report. It was short, and it was honest. Then she sat in the cockpit and waited for a reply that did not come.

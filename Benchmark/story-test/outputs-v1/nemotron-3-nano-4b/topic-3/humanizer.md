The air in Harrow Creek was thick with the scent of damp earth and diesel, the kind that clung to everything after a long winter. At 12:14 a.m., the static of WKRV’s midnight show crackled into life, and Dale Ransome’s voice, worn smooth by eleven years of late-night broadcasts, filled the quiet studio. He was the only voice most people trusted after dark, the steady hand on the dial when the world outside went silent. And he took every call seriously, even the ones that made no sense.  

The caller came in at 12:14. Her name was June. She didn’t give a last name, didn’t explain herself, just said, “I can tell you what the weather will be like tomorrow.” Dale blinked. “You can?” he asked, voice low. “You’re on the air. You’re not a weather machine.” But he let her talk. She said the rain would start at the water tower around six in the morning, that the wind would turn east by noon, and that the hail would come just after three. She sounded calm, almost bored, like she was reading a shopping list. “Rain at six,” she said. “Wind east at noon.” “Hail at three.” “That’s it.” No more. No less.  

Dale felt a shift in the room. Not a physical one, but a quiet one, like the moment before a storm breaks. He checked the forecast sheet on his desk. It said clear skies. He stared at it. The words felt like a lie. The station hummed. At the end of the day, he decided to play the call the next morning. Not to prove her right, but to see if the sky would answer.  

The next morning, the rain began at 6:02 at the water tower. The wind turned east by eleven. The hail came at 3:10. Dale sat in his car in the station lot, a thermos of coffee cooling beside him, watching the sky turn grey. He had no explanation. He didn’t pretend to have one. He just listened. The rain tapped the roof. The wind whispered through the trees. The hail fell like tiny stones, silent and sudden.  

June never called back.  

The station received a few emails. A flood of questions. “Did you hear that?” “Was it real?” “Did you know?” One letter arrived with no return address. It was folded small, tucked inside a book of old poetry. Dale kept it in his drawer, next to the old forecast sheet, the one that said clear skies. He never again said the word clear on air. Not once.  

That night, the radio played on. Dale’s voice, steady and calm, filled the silence. “Good evening, Harrow Creek. This is Dale Ransome. The weather is… uncertain.” He paused. “But I’ll be here when you’re ready.”

The drought had lasted three years, and the rooftop-garden co-op on Grey Stair had felt every week of it. It was a small building, twelve flats stacked on a slope, but it had its own life. At the top, a large concrete cistern—tank—held water that fed all of them. It was shared, always shared, a quiet promise between neighbors. The co-op had never needed a key, never needed a receipt, never needed to worry. The water came, and it was theirs.

When the notice arrived, Mara Ellis read it twice. A developer named Lindqvist Partners had purchased the rights to the tank. The letter was polite. It said the rights would be "managed going forward" and that access would be "reviewed in due course." Mara felt a knot in her stomach. The co-op had navigated many complexities over the years—shared gardens, shared tools, shared silence during the long dry spells—but this felt like a game-changer. It wasn’t just a tank. It was a lifeline. And now, it might be gone.

At the Thursday meeting, the members gathered around the tea urn. Everyone had something to say. Old Tobin said he had seen this coming. Priya said they should call a lawyer. Mara said nothing for a while, then spoke. "We have the tank key, the pump receipts, and nine years of meter readings. That counts for something." The room went quiet. It was a testament to her steady nature that people listened. She didn’t shout. She didn’t cry. She just stated the facts, like a tide returning to shore.

They decided to shed light on the situation. They photographed the meters, copied the receipts, and posted the timeline on the stairwell wall. By the weekend, the whole street knew. The water was not just a resource. It was a story. And the story was being written in ink and memory.

The tank was not just concrete and steel. It was the sound of the pump humming at dawn, the smell of wet soil after a rare rain, the way the water pooled in the corner of the rooftop garden, where the tomatoes grew tall and the basil smelled like summer. It was the way the co-op had always worked—without asking, without waiting. They had shared it because they had to. Because they were neighbors. Because they were home.

Now, the tank was being sold. The rights were in the hands of Lindqvist Partners. The key was being handed over. The pump receipts were being archived. The meter readings were being filed. The timeline was posted, but the water was not.

Mara stood at the edge of the stairwell, watching the sun dip behind the buildings. A child ran past, laughing, chasing a balloon. The tank was gone. But the memory remained. The co-op had shared the tank. They had shared the water. They had shared the silence of the drought, the joy of the rain, the quiet pride of growing food together.

And when the next dry spell came, the water would come again. Not from a developer. Not from a key. But from the sky. From the earth. From the shared tank, still standing, still full, still theirs.

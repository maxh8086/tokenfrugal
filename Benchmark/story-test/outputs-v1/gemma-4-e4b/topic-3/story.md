The air in the WKRV studio was thick with the low, comforting hum of late-night radio. In the small, unassuming town of Harrow Creek, this midnight slot was more than just entertainment; it was a fixture, a quiet anchor in the vast, unpredictable expanse of the night. For eleven years, Dale Ransome had been the voice of that anchor. He hosted the show, a solitary sentinel against the encroaching darkness, and he took every call seriously. Whether it was a late-night confession, a lost pet sighting, or a rambling anecdote, Dale treated each transmission with the same measured gravity.

It was 12:14 AM when the call came through. The voice on the other end was soft, almost unnervingly placid. The caller identified herself only as June.

“I can describe tomorrow’s weather,” she stated, her tone utterly devoid of drama.

Dale, seasoned by years of the bizarre, felt a familiar prickle of skepticism, but he allowed her to continue. He leaned into the microphone, the sound of his own breathing suddenly amplified in the quiet booth.

June proceeded to list the details with the detached precision of someone reading a grocery list. “The rain,” she murmured, “will start at the water tower around six in the morning. Then, the wind will turn east by noon. And the hail,” she paused, a slight, almost bored inflection entering her voice, “will come just after three.”

When she finished, the line clicked dead.

Dale sat back in his chair, the silence rushing back in to fill the void. He felt a distinct shift in the atmosphere of the small studio, a subtle vibration that had nothing to do with the aging equipment. He reached for the laminated forecast sheet on his desk. It glowed under the desk lamp, proclaiming a perfect, unblemished slate: *Clear Skies*.

He stared at the paper, then at the phone, then back at the paper. The hum of the studio seemed to deepen, a low, expectant thrum. He wrestled with the discrepancy for the rest of the night. Logic demanded he dismiss it as coincidence, a fanciful delusion born of insomnia. But the sheer, unwavering calm in June’s voice suggested something else entirely. By the time the early morning shift began, Dale made a decision. He would play the call.

The next day, the world outside the station felt suddenly charged.

At 6:02 AM, precisely, the first fat drops of rain began to spatter against the metal of the water tower. It wasn't a gentle drizzle; it was the start of a determined downpour.

Dale, unable to shake the feeling, drove his old sedan out to the station lot. He parked near the edge of the gravel, watching the sky as the morning progressed. By 11:00 AM, the wind, which had been a steady, southerly push, abruptly shifted, sighing eastward across the fields.

He waited, the rain softening to a steady drumming rhythm. The afternoon dragged, heavy and expectant. Then, just after 3:00 PM, the sky turned a bruised, violent grey. The hail arrived not with a gentle patter, but with a sharp, rattling percussion, peppering the asphalt and the leaves in sudden, icy bursts.

Dale sat in his car, the drumming of the hail a deafening soundtrack to his quiet realization. He had no explanation. There was no meteorological anomaly, no atmospheric pressure system that could account for the precise timing of June’s pronouncements. He did not, and could not, pretend to have one.

June never called back.

The aftermath was a strange, quiet storm of its own. The station received a flood of emails, a deluge of questions from listeners who had been tuned in, demanding to know how the forecast had been so perfectly, impossibly accurate. There was also a single, thick letter, addressed to no one in particular, found tucked into the mail slot—it bore no return address.

Dale did not try to decipher the letter. He simply placed it in a drawer, beside the brittle, optimistic forecast sheet from the previous night. From that day forward, the word "clear" vanished from his broadcast. The midnight voice of WKRV remained, but it carried a new, unspoken weight—the weight of a perfect, inexplicable prediction.

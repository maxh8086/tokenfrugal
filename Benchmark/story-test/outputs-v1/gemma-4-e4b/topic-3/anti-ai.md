The air in the WKRV studio held the low, comforting hum of late-night radio. In Harrow Creek, this midnight slot served as a quiet anchor against the night's vast, unpredictable expanse. For eleven years, Dale Ransome had been the voice of that anchor. He hosted the show, a solitary sentinel, treating every call with measured gravity. Whether a late-night confession, a lost pet sighting, or a rambling anecdote, Dale gave each transmission his full attention.

At 12:14 AM, the call connected. The voice on the other end was soft, almost unnervingly placid. The caller identified herself only as June.

“I can describe tomorrow’s weather,” she stated, her tone utterly devoid of drama.

Dale, accustomed to the strange, felt a familiar prickle of doubt, but he let her continue. He leaned into the microphone, the sound of his own breathing suddenly amplified in the quiet booth.

June listed the details with detached precision. “The rain,” she murmured, “will start at the water tower around six in the morning. Then, the wind will turn east by noon. And the hail,” she paused, a slight, almost bored inflection entering her voice, “will come just after three.”

When she finished, the line went dead.

Dale sat back, the silence rushing in. He felt a distinct shift in the studio's atmosphere, a subtle vibration unrelated to the aging equipment. He reached for the laminated forecast sheet on his desk. It glowed under the desk lamp, proclaiming a perfect, unblemished slate: *Clear Skies*.

He stared at the paper, then the phone, then the paper again. The studio hum deepened, a low, expectant thrum. He wrestled with the discrepancy all night. Logic suggested coincidence, a fanciful delusion born of insomnia. Yet, the unwavering calm in June’s voice suggested something else. By the time the early morning shift began, Dale decided he must share the call.

The next day, the world outside the station felt suddenly charged.

At 6:02 AM, precisely, the first fat drops of rain spattered against the water tower metal. It was not a gentle drizzle; it was the start of a determined downpour.

Dale, unable to shake the feeling, drove his old sedan to the station lot. He parked near the gravel edge, watching the sky as morning passed. By 11:00 AM, the wind, which had been a steady, southerly push, abruptly shifted, sighing eastward across the fields.

He waited, the rain softening to a steady drumming rhythm. The afternoon dragged, heavy and expectant. Then, just after 3:00 PM, the sky turned a bruised, violent grey. The hail arrived not with a gentle patter, but with a sharp, rattling percussion, peppering the asphalt and leaves in sudden, icy bursts.

Dale sat in his car, the hail’s drumming a deafening soundtrack to his quiet realization. He had no explanation. No meteorological anomaly, no pressure system could account for the precise timing of June’s pronouncements. He could not pretend to have one.

June never called back.

The aftermath was a strange, quiet storm. The station received a flood of emails, questions from listeners who had tuned in, demanding to know how the forecast had been so perfectly accurate. Also found in the mail slot was a single, thick letter, addressed to no one in particular, bearing no return address.

Dale did not try to decipher the letter. He placed it in a drawer, beside the brittle, optimistic forecast sheet from the previous night. From that day forward, the word "clear" vanished from his broadcast. The midnight voice of WKRV remained, but it carried a new, unspoken weight—the weight of a perfect, inexplicable prediction.

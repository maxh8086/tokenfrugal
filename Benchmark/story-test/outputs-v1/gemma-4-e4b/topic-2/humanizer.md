The northern corridor was Ines Varga’s domain. For six years, the automated freight drone’s hum had been her constant companion, a predictable rhythm mirroring her own movements. She knew the route’s contours—the subtle pressure shifts over glacial peaks, the magnetic pull near the eastern waypoint, the exact moment solar arrays entered shadow. The manifest was law: medical stock, spare parts. That was the agreement, the fixed truth of her flight plan.

Tuesday should have been routine. The drone lifted from the loading bay, the approved cargo settling into the hold. But as sensors cycled diagnostics, a jarring note entered her routine. The load sensor registered four sealed boxes.

They were foreign.

They lacked listing. They did not match the manifest she signed, the document underpinning her career. On the dispatch screen, they displayed a cryptic tag: "reserved cargo."

A cold tension tightened in Ines’s stomach. This was not a malfunction; it was an infiltration. Something moved through her established path, clandestine cargo entering the flow, and no one—not ground crew, not dispatch—had alerted her.

Ines, a woman whose life relied on verifiable data, approached the anomaly with careful precision. She did not panic; she documented. She checked the seal numbers on the four boxes, noting the unbroken integrity of the tamper-proof seals. She cross-referenced the flight log, scrutinizing every timestamp, every altitude reading, searching for when the unauthorized weight was added.

The boxes felt strange. A deep, pervasive chill emanated from them, defying the cargo bay’s temperature. They also vibrated. It was not the drone’s engine thrum; it was a low, steady resonance, a frequency filling the small cabin, a quiet murmur beneath the drone’s operational sound.

When the drone docked at the next depot, Ines addressed the loader, a man whose face usually held the practiced neutrality of maintenance.

"These four boxes," she stated, pointing to the readout, "they are not on the manifest."

The loader looked at the screen, then at her. He offered a slight shrug. "Not my area to question, Captain."

She moved to the dispatch terminal. The dispatcher, whose voice was usually sharp and efficient, paused when Ines showed the discrepancy.

"These were cleared," the dispatcher murmured, tapping her console. "The flight plan received clearance from oversight."

"Oversight," Ines repeated, the word tasting like ash. She leaned back in her pilot’s chair, the boxes’ vibration traveling through the seat. She considered that phrase for a long time. Oversight. A bureaucratic screen over an unapproved payload.

She piloted the aircraft. She held the flight’s integrity. She would answer when failure arrived. The weight of that duty, suddenly increased by this unknown cargo, settled heavily on her.

She filed a report. It was brief, exact, and truthful. She detailed the boxes, the vibration, the mismatch, and the vague mention of "oversight" clearance. She submitted it, clicked 'send,' and returned to the cockpit.

The drone powered up, the familiar ascent beginning. Ines watched the northern corridor unfold below, the peaks blurring. She waited for the reply, the official response, the order to continue or stop.

The silence that followed was total. The automated systems functioned perfectly; the drone held its trajectory. The four cold, humming boxes remained, a secret riding silently through the vast, empty sky.

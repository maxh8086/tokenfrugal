The late-night radio landscape in Harrow Creek was a quiet, isolated thing, a solitary pulse against the vast darkness of the Midwest. In this small inland town, WKRV's midnight show was the only voice many people trusted after dark, a comforting hum that cut through the silence of empty streets and sleeping houses. For eleven years, Dale Ransome had run the show, his voice a steady anchor in a world that felt increasingly fragile. Dale took every call seriously, a habit born of necessity and a deep respect for the people who reached out in the dead of night. It didn't matter if the caller was a neighbor complaining about a leaky faucet or someone speaking in riddles about the stars; Dale listened with the intensity of a man holding a lifeline.

It was 12:14 AM when the phone rang, a sharp, electric intrusion into Dale's routine. The caller identified herself only as June. Her voice was clear, devoid of the usual tremor of fear or urgency that characterized most late-night callers. She began to speak with a strange, rhythmic precision. "Tomorrow," she said, her tone flat and matter-of-fact, "the rain will start at the water tower around six in the morning. The wind will turn east by noon, and the hail will come just after three." She paused, taking a breath that sounded almost bored, as if she were simply reading off a grocery list rather than predicting a storm.

Dale felt an immediate, physical shift in the room. The air in the studio seemed to grow heavier, the hum of the equipment sounding slightly off-key. He glanced down at the forecast sheet pinned to his desk, his eyes scanning the columns of data. The report clearly stated clear skies for the next twenty-four hours. The numbers were consistent, the models aligned. Yet, as June continued to speak with that eerie calm, Dale found himself unable to dismiss her. He thought about the nature of the town, how isolated it was, how often the weather seemed to defy logic in the winter months. Driven by a mix of skepticism and an inexplicable pull, he decided to play the call the next morning. He would let the world hear what June had to say.

The next day dawned with a deceptive brightness, but by 6:02 AM, the sky darkened instantly. The rain began exactly at the water tower, soaking the concrete and the grass in a sudden, cold deluge. Dale sat in his car in the station lot, watching the grey clouds roll in, his heart pounding a frantic rhythm against his ribs. He had no explanation for the phenomenon, and he did not pretend to have one. He simply watched the wind pick up, turning east by eleven, and then, at 3:10 PM, the hail struck with a rhythmic, stinging violence. The sequence was perfect, a mirror image of June's words.

In the days that followed, the town buzzed with a mixture of awe and confusion. June never called back. The station received a flood of emails from residents who claimed they had heard the prediction, and a few curious visitors stopped by the studio to ask questions. But the most haunting artifact of the event was a single letter that arrived in the mail, addressed to Dale but bearing no return address. It was a simple sheet of paper, perhaps written by June herself, containing no explanation, only a silence that spoke louder than any forecast. Dale kept the letter in his drawer, tucking it next to the old, crumpled forecast sheet that had once declared clear skies. He never opened it again. From that day forward, Dale Ransome changed his ways. He never again said the word "clear" on air, knowing that sometimes, the truth is not found in the data, but in the voices that dare to speak when the world is asleep.

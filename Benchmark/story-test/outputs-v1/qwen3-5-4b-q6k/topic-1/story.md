The sun beat down on the grey concrete of Port Marrow, a coastal city that had forgotten how to hold water. For three years, the drought had been a relentless, parching hand, squeezing the life out of the streets and the soil. On Grey Stair, the rooftop-garden co-op had felt every week of this absence, watching their beloved rooftop garden wither into brown, brittle husks. It was a community built on cooperation, a tight-knit group of twelve families who had always shared the building's tank: a massive, weathered concrete cistern perched precariously on the roof, its pipes feeding every flat in the tower.

The silence in Mara Ellis's flat was broken only by the frantic tapping of her pen against the paper. She had received the notice twice, reading the same words over and over until they felt like a foreign language. A developer named Lindqvist Partners had purchased the rights to the tank. The letter was deceptively polite, written in the smooth, corporate font of a corporation that cared nothing for the people it displaced. It stated that the rights would be "managed going forward" and that access would be "reviewed in due course." Mara felt a tight, cold knot forming in her stomach. The co-op had navigated many complexities over the years—disputes over rent, arguments about garden maintenance, the quiet friction of shared living—but this felt like a game-changer. This wasn't just a disagreement; it was an eviction of their lifeline.

The Thursday meeting was held in the main hall, the air thick with the scent of damp tea leaves and rising anxiety. The members gathered around the large, silver tea urn, steam curling into the dim light. Everyone had something to say, voices overlapping in a chorus of fear and indignation. Old Tobin, a man whose back was bent by decades of climbing stairs, spoke first. He had seen this coming, he said, his voice raspy. "They bought the building's soul," he muttered, gesturing vaguely toward the ceiling. "They'll take the water, and then they'll take the rest."

Priya, sharp-eyed and quick-witted, immediately countered. "We can't just sit here and wait for permission. We should call a lawyer. We need to send a formal demand letter." The room buzzed with agreement. Mara, however, said nothing for a long while. She watched the steam rise, her mind racing through the history of their building. She remembered the way the pump hummed in the morning, the way the water level on the gauge dipped slowly during the dry spells. She remembered the trust they had placed in the system. Finally, she spoke, her voice steady and clear, cutting through the panic. "We have the tank key, the pump receipts, and nine years of meter readings. That counts for something."

The room went quiet. The air seemed to hold its breath. It was a testament to her steady nature that people listened, even when the fear was palpable. They were not just arguing about a resource; they were arguing about their identity as a community. Mara's words shifted the dynamic from panic to strategy. They were not helpless victims; they were holders of evidence.

They decided to shed light on the situation, turning the private dispute into a public matter. Mara took the camera from her bag, her hands steady despite the tremor in her heart. They photographed the meters, capturing the numbers that proved the co-op's usage and the tank's capacity. They copied the receipts, highlighting the dates and the costs, creating a paper trail that was impossible to ignore. Together, they posted the timeline on the stairwell wall, a large sheet of paper pinned to the concrete with heavy-duty tape. The document listed the purchase date, the terms of the letter, and the co-op's response.

By the weekend, the whole street knew. The notice on the wall had become a focal point, a symbol of resistance against the encroaching corporate machine. Neighbors stopped to read the paper, pointing at the dates and the names. The drought had been a test of endurance, but the loss of the water rights was a test of unity. Mara stood by the wall, watching the street below, knowing that while the tank might be in the hands of a developer, the spirit of the co-op on Grey Stair was no longer dry.

High windows let morning light spill across the Corvane Logistics headquarters, where servers hummed to keep the city's supply chain moving. Inside the main control room, the Fleet Planner sat like a silent, metallic god, built specifically to reason through the labyrinthine complexity of routing problems. It did not guess; it reasoned, step by step, with a cold, logical precision that made human error seem almost quaint. However, its wisdom was strictly bounded by the rules it inherited from the past.

The planner's internal logic began with a deceptively simple premise: the Eastmoor depot had a loading capacity of forty pallets per shift. It absorbed this fact as an absolute truth. Immediately, it moved to the next step: twelve trucks currently queued on the coast road were being rerouted away from that route. The logic flowed seamlessly into the third step. If Eastmoor could only take forty pallets, then the overflow must move to the Western Yard. Since the Western Yard route was shorter, the system concluded the fleet should switch immediately. The output was a tidy, robust chain of reasoning that seemed unassailable—a perfect loop of cause and effect.

It was Analyst Renata Osei who first noticed the dissonance. She arrived at her desk on a Monday morning, the usual time for reviewing the day's logistics. As she examined the old rule book, page by page, her fingers paused on a section marked "Historical Constraints." Something felt off about the efficiency of the new plan. She dug deeper, tracing the lineage of the rules back to their origin. There, buried in a digital archive from six years earlier, she found the original note.

The note confirmed what the planner had assumed: Eastmoor's capacity was indeed forty pallets per shift. But the context was missing. The note specified that this capacity applied only to the north dock. The south dock had been closed since a devastating flood six years prior, rendering it unusable for loading. The planner, lacking the historical memory of the flood, had no way of knowing the south dock was closed. It simply took the total number and applied it to the entire facility.

Renata felt a chill run down her spine. She understood the nature of the failure now. The planner's conclusion was not wrong in its logic; the steps were flawless. It was wrong in its first premise, and every subsequent step inherited that fatal mistake. The chain of reasoning had been pivotal to the decision, guiding the fleet away from a viable route, yet it had been built on a single false line in a document nobody had reread in years.

With a steady hand, Renata updated the rule in the system, adding the crucial caveat about the south dock's closure. She reran the planner, watching the interface update in real-time. The logic engine recalculated, its internal chain shifting to accommodate the new truth. The twelve trucks, previously ordered to leave the coast road, were now instructed to turn back. The system corrected its course, and the trucks began their journey toward the coast road once more, their drivers unaware of the six-year-old flood that had nearly derailed the entire operation. Renata watched the trucks turn back, knowing that while the machine was powerful, it remained entirely dependent on the human stories it was fed.
